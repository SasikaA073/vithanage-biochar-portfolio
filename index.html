How this was put together, and its limits

Every item on the Google Scholar profile was read. Items were kept when the title or abstract mentioned biochar, hydrochar or a biomass-derived char. Duplicate listings, a correction notice and items only loosely related to biochar were removed. Reviews on carbon-based adsorbents in general and on organic amendments for petroleum-contaminated soil were also left out, though they touch on biochar.

Claude read each remaining title and abstract (where one was available) and recorded:

  • Output type: research article, review, book chapter, book, conference or proceedings item, or student thesis or report.
  • Medium: water, soil, both, or other (production, materials, agronomy).
  • Feedstock: the biomass the biochar was made from, when stated.
  • Modification: any treatment beyond plain pyrolysis, such as steam or acid activation, clay composites, chitosan coating, nitrogen doping or milling to colloidal or nano size.
  • Contaminants: each named target, grouped into classes and marked organic or inorganic. Broad terms such as "metals" or "antibiotics" count toward a class but not toward the named-contaminant total.

Limits: about a third of the items had no abstract available, so those were classified from the title alone. Labels are based only on what the title or abstract states. Where a paper says only "woody biochar" or "invasive plant", the feedstock is recorded without a species and is not counted as a distinct feedstock, and feedstocks or modifications not mentioned at all are missed. Conference abstracts often repeat a journal paper, so counts that include them overstate distinct studies; the peer-reviewed count leaves them out. Google Scholar citation counts run higher than Scopus or Web of Science. The full classification can be downloaded and checked row by row from the data files linked at the foot of this page.
